When people say they want a “real blog,” they often mean they want a CMS. What they usually need is a stable publishing loop.

Those are not the same thing.

A CMS is useful when you have multiple editors, a review process, structured content dependencies, or a strong need for non-technical publishing. None of those conditions apply to most personal sites. A personal blog usually has one author, a small number of content types, and a strong need for portability.

That is why I start with Markdown.

Markdown has a few advantages that matter immediately:

- The writing stays readable in raw form.
- Version control becomes automatic.
- Moving between frameworks is dramatically easier.
- The content model stays honest because you can see it directly.

There is also a psychological advantage. Markdown keeps the publishing act close to the writing act. There are fewer panels, fewer configuration detours, and fewer decisions that pretend to be productive. You write, commit, preview, publish.

This does not mean CMS tools are bad. It means they solve a later problem. They become worth the complexity when the bottleneck shifts from authoring to coordination. If the bottleneck is still “I do not publish consistently,” then adding a CMS often creates the wrong kind of sophistication.

The rule I like is simple: choose the smallest system that makes publishing feel inevitable. For a single-author blog, Markdown wins that argument more often than people admit.

When the constraints change, you can migrate. That option is exactly why starting with portable files is such a good default.
